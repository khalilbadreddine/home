# The Entire History of Japan

## 1. Hook

In 1853, four black ships sailed into Tokyo Bay. Japan had kept its doors almost shut for more than two hundred years. Fifteen years later, its government was gone. Less than a century after that, its cities were ash. And twenty-three years after that, it was the second-largest economy on Earth.

No country has rebuilt itself so often, so fast, or so completely.

So how does a chain of islands on the edge of the map keep reinventing itself? Why did a ruling class give up its swords by law? And why does Japan now have about nine million empty houses?

This is the story of a queen who spoke to spirits, a lady of the court who wrote the world's first great novel, a warlord betrayed inside a burning temple, a shogun who ordered the sea closed, and a train that left the station exactly on time.

And all of it comes down to one habit. Japan opens the door, borrows everything, and turns it into something its own. And sometimes, it slams the door shut.

## 2. The islands

Japan is not one island. It is nearly fourteen thousand of them, strung along the edge of the Pacific. About three quarters of the land is mountains. Earthquakes, volcanoes, typhoons. Remember that, because it shapes everything.

People lived here more than thirty thousand years ago. By fifteen thousand years ago, the Jōmon were making pottery, some of the oldest on Earth, decorated with cords pressed into wet clay.

Around 300 BC, newcomers arrived from the Korean peninsula with wet-rice farming, bronze, and iron. Rice meant surplus. And surplus meant rulers.

## 3. Queens and tombs

Chinese chroniclers described a land of warring chiefs, until the fighting stopped, when they agreed on a queen. Her name was Himiko. She was said to rule through spirits, and almost never be seen. Around the year 238, she sent envoys to China, and they came home with a gold seal.

Historians still argue about where her kingdom, Yamatai, actually stood.

Later came the tombs. Giant keyhole-shaped mounds, ringed with moats. The largest, near Osaka, is almost five hundred meters long. By then one clan, the Yamato, was winning. Their chiefs would come to be called emperors.

## 4. Borrowing China

Japan looked across the sea at the greatest civilization it knew, and took notes. Writing. Law. Buddhism, in the 500s. In 604, Prince Shōtoku set out a constitution of seventeen articles, and sent students to China.

In 710, the court built its first lasting capital at Nara, laid out like the Chinese capital, grid and all. In 794, it moved again, to a new city called Heian-kyō. Today we call it Kyoto. And the emperor would live there for more than a thousand years.

Then something odd happened. Japan stopped copying and started creating. Court women developed a way to write in Japanese. And around the year 1000, one of them, Murasaki Shikibu, wrote The Tale of Genji, often called the world's first novel.

It was a world of poems, perfume, and layered silk. So refined, that the fighting was left to the provinces.

## 5. The rise of the samurai

But in those provinces, warriors were getting stronger. In 1185, after a bitter civil war, the Minamoto clan won. In 1192, their leader, Yoritomo, took the title shogun, which means general, and ruled from Kamakura, far from the court.

Now Japan had two rulers. An emperor who reigned, and a shogun who ruled. That strange arrangement would last almost seven hundred years.

Then came the Mongols. In 1274, and again in 1281, Kublai Khan sent huge fleets. The samurai fought on the beaches, and storms wrecked much of the second fleet. Later Japanese called those storms kamikaze, the divine wind. Hold on to that word.

## 6. A country at war

By the 1400s, the shogun's grip had failed. In 1467, a dispute over succession turned Kyoto into a battlefield, and for the next hundred years warlords fought over everything. This age is called Sengoku, the country at war.

Then, in 1543, a ship carrying Portuguese traders was blown ashore on a small island. They had muskets. Within a few years, Japanese blacksmiths were copying them.

In 1575, a warlord named Oda Nobunaga placed his gunners behind wooden fences at Nagashino, and shattered a famous cavalry. He was close to uniting Japan, when, in 1582, one of his own generals attacked him inside a temple in Kyoto. Nobunaga died in the flames.

A peasant's son, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, finished the job. He took swords away from the farmers, and then overreached, invading Korea twice. When he died in 1598, he left a child heir and many rivals.

In October 1600, at Sekigahara, Tokugawa Ieyasu won the biggest battle in samurai history. In 1603, he became shogun.

## 7. Two hundred years of peace

Ieyasu wanted one thing. No more war. He made a fishing town called Edo his capital. Today it is Tokyo. Every regional lord had to spend every other year there, leaving their families behind as hostages. Roads were built. Merchants grew rich. And Edo grew to about a million people, one of the largest cities on Earth.

Then the shogunate grew afraid of foreign influence, and of Christianity. By the 1630s, foreigners were expelled, and Japanese were forbidden to leave. Only a few Dutch and Chinese traders were allowed, on one tiny artificial island in Nagasaki, called Dejima.

For more than two hundred years, there was almost no war. Kabuki theaters, woodblock prints, haiku, sumo. A culture of the floating world.

But the world outside was changing, and Japan wasn't watching. Remember those black ships.

## 8. The black ships

In July 1853, Commodore Matthew Perry steamed into Tokyo Bay with four warships, black with coal smoke, and a letter demanding that Japan open its ports. The shogun had no navy that could answer.

Fifteen years later, his government collapsed. In 1868, young samurai from outlying regions restored power to a fifteen-year-old emperor, Meiji. And then they did something extraordinary. They dismantled their own class. Samurai lost their pay, and in 1876, their right to carry swords.

A modern army. Railways. Schools. A constitution. All of it copied from the best the West had to offer, and built at breakneck speed. In 1895, Japan beat China. In 1905, it shocked the world by beating Russia.

## 9. Empire and war

But Japan had learned the West's lessons too well, including empire. It took Taiwan. It annexed Korea in 1910. It seized Manchuria in 1931, and in 1937 invaded China, where its army committed terrible atrocities, including the Nanjing massacre.

In December 1941, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, and a war it could not win spread across the Pacific. By 1945, American bombers had burned most of its major cities. On August 6, an atomic bomb destroyed Hiroshima. Three days later, Nagasaki.

On August 15, for the first time, the Japanese people heard their emperor's voice, announcing surrender. About three million Japanese had died, and many more people across Asia.

## 10. The rebuild

Under American occupation, Japan got a new constitution. The emperor became a symbol, and Article 9 renounced war.

Then came the miracle. Cars, electronics, and the work of millions. In October 1964, just days before the Tokyo Olympics, the world's first high-speed train, the Shinkansen, left Tokyo for Osaka. By 1968, Japan had the second-largest economy on Earth.

In the 1980s, the boom became a bubble. At the end of 1989, the stock market peaked. Then it crashed, and growth stalled for decades.

In March 2011, a magnitude nine earthquake and tsunami left about eighteen thousand people dead or missing and triggered a nuclear disaster at Fukushima. And again, Japan rebuilt.

## 11. Why it matters

So where is Japan now? Its population peaked around 2008. In 2024, fewer than 700,000 babies were born, the lowest number on record, while nearly three in ten people are over sixty-five. Some villages have closed their last school. About nine million homes sit empty.

Japan is again standing at the door, deciding how much of the world to let in.

Japan has been told its time was over before. By black ships. By a bomb. By a bubble. And each time it did the same thing. It looked outward, borrowed what worked, kept what mattered, and built something unmistakably its own.

A queen who spoke to spirits. A shogun who closed the sea. Samurai who gave up their own swords. And a train that left on time.

The door has been open, shut, and open again. The question is what Japan builds next.
